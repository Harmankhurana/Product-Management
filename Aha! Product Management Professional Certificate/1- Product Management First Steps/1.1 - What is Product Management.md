# Lecture Notes: Types of Products

**Course:** Product Management: First Steps | **Topic:** What counts as a product

### Summary

A product is not just a physical object. Anything that has to be **designed, built, tested, and released** is a product, including software, services, technical rules, and even the process used to make something. A PM must be able to recognize which *type* of product they own, because each type has different customers, risks, and success measures.

### Core idea

> **Product = anything that is designed, built, tested, and released to serve a need.**
> 

This is why the PM’s job is the same in spirit whether the product is an app, a chip, or a factory process.

### The six types of products

| # | Type | What it is | Who “uses” it | Smartphone example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **User product** | The complete thing an end user directly uses | End customer | The phone itself |
| 2 | **Component product** | A part that does nothing alone but is essential to something bigger | Other product teams | Processor, sensors, screen, storage |
| 3 | **Platform product** | A base layer that connects other products and lets them work together | Developers and other products | iOS, Android |
| 4 | **Service** | Behind-the-scenes functionality that products call on (the “roads and transit hubs”) | Apps and systems | Cloud storage backend, messaging, photo upload |
| 5 | **Standard** | Agreed-upon technical rules everyone follows so systems can interoperate | The entire industry | IP, TCP |
| 6 | **Process** | The way a product is built, manufactured, and assembled | The company itself | The phone’s manufacturing and assembly line |

### Each type explained

#### 1. User product

The finished, visible product a person actually picks up or opens. Everything else exists to make this work.

#### 2. Component product

Each component must itself be designed, built, tested, and released. It is a product in its own right, but its “customer” is usually another team, not an end user. Components are dependent: they only come alive when combined with other products.

#### 3. Platform product

A platform is the foundation that other products are built on. The operating system connects the hardware to the apps and interface you see. Many products depend on one platform, so a platform PM serves developers and partner teams as much as end users.

#### 4. Services

Services are invisible infrastructure. When you send an email, text, or upload a photo, one or more services work behind the scenes. Like roads and transit hubs, you only notice them when they fail.

#### 5. Standards

Standards are the shared rules of the technical world. For example:

- **IP (Internet Protocol):** defines how data is addressed and routed.
- **TCP (Transmission Control Protocol):** defines how data is broken into pieces, sent, validated, and accepted at the destination.

They are products too: someone had to design, test, and release them.

#### 6. Process

How a product is made is also a product. The assembly line, supply chain, and quality testing flow all needed to be designed, tested, and rolled out.

### Worked example from the lecture: an airplane

| Type | Airplane example |
| --- | --- |
| User product | The plane itself (what the passenger uses) |
| Component | The engine |
| Platform | The in-flight entertainment system, which works the same across every seat |
| Service | Radios and GPS that connect to other radios and satellites |
| Standard | Rules ensuring planes from different manufacturers work with any airport or airline |
| Process | The manufacturing and assembly of the plane |

### Added examples *(not from the lecture)*

These show the same framework applied to everyday products.

**Swiggy / Zomato (food delivery)**

- **User product:** the customer app
- **Component:** the payment SDK or map widget inside the app
- **Platform:** the delivery-partner network and APIs that restaurants plug into
- **Service:** order-routing, notifications, live tracking
- **Standard:** UPI for payments
- **Process:** the restaurant onboarding and quality audit workflow

**Gmail**

- **User product:** the inbox you use
- **Platform:** Google account and sign-in used across Google apps
- **Service:** spam filtering, storage
- **Standard:** SMTP, the protocol that lets Gmail talk to any other email provider

**Electric car**

- **User product:** the car
- **Component:** the battery pack
- **Standard:** charging connector standards (e.g., CCS)
- **Process:** the gigafactory production line

### Why this matters for a PM

- **Know your customer.** A component PM’s customer may be another engineering team; a user-product PM’s customer is a consumer. The same job looks very different.
- **Recognize dependencies.** User products rely on components, platforms, services, and standards. A failure in a service (say, a cloud outage) breaks the user product even if the app itself is perfect.
- **Widen your career view.** PM roles exist for all six types, not just consumer apps. Platform, infrastructure, and process PM roles are common in tech companies.
- **The job doesn’t change:** a PM ensures their product, *regardless of type*, is **designed, built, tested, and released**.

### Key takeaways

1. Products are not always physical objects.
2. There are six types: user, component, platform, service, standard, and process.
3. Most real-world products are a **stack** of several types working together.
4. Anything you design, build, test, and release is a product, even something invisible like a protocol.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

- ❌ “A product must be a physical thing or an app.” Services, standards, and processes count too.
- ❌ “Components and services aren’t real products because users never see them.” They still need product management; their customers are just internal or technical.
- ❌ Confusing platform and service. A **platform** is something other products are *built on* (iOS). A **service** is something products *call on to get work done* (a cloud storage backend).

## Lecture Notes: Product vs. Project Management

**Course:** Product Management: First Steps | **Topic:** How product management differs from project management

### Summary

Product management and project management are related but distinct disciplines. **Product management** makes sure you are building the *right thing*: something people want, find valuable, or that integrates well with other products. **Project management** makes sure the thing *can actually be built*: on time, within budget, with the available people. One looks outward at users, the other looks inward at the team.

### Core idea

> **Product management = “What should we build, and why?”**
> 
> 
> **Project management = “Can we build it, and how and when?”**
> 

### Side-by-side comparison

| Aspect | Product Management | Project Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Core question** | Is this valuable and wanted? | Is this feasible to deliver? |
| **Focus** | Value, usefulness, user needs | Schedule, resources, budget |
| **Perspective** | **Outward**: users, customers, dependent components | **Inward**: the team building the product |
| **Main job** | Gather needs and translate them into features for the team | Schedule work, monitor progress, report back |
| **Success looks like** | People use and value the product | Work delivered on time, within budget and resources |
| **Failure looks like** | Built something nobody wants | Built the right thing, but late or over budget |

### The “circle” model from the lecture

Imagine the product as a circle:

- **Outside the circle:** users, customers, and other products or components that must work with yours. Their needs flow *in*.
- **Inside the circle:** the engineers, designers, and others who build the product.

**Product management** stands at the boundary and translates outside needs into features the team can build.

**Project management** works inside, planning the work, tracking how the team is doing, and communicating progress back to the product manager.

Both roles together close the loop: needs go in, a built product comes out, and status flows back.

### Do you need two separate people?

Not necessarily. The lecture makes three points:

1. A team does **not** need both a product manager and a project manager.
2. Often the **product manager does both**, working externally (users) and internally (team).
3. Project management is sometimes done by someone **without the title**, such as an **engineering manager**.

The key point: the *discipline* matters more than the *job title*. Someone has to do both jobs, whoever it is.

### Example *(not from the lecture)*

**Scenario: building a “Schedule Order” feature for a food delivery app**

- **Product management asks:**
    - Do users actually want to schedule meals in advance?
    - Which users (office workers, busy parents)?
    - What should the feature do, and how will we know it works?
    - *Output:* a defined feature with clear user value.
- **Project management asks:**
    - How many engineer-weeks will this take?
    - Does it conflict with the payments migration happening the same quarter?
    - Can we ship before the festive-season rush?
    - *Output:* a schedule, resource plan, and progress updates.

**What goes wrong without each:**

- *Strong project management, weak product management:* the team ships on time, but nobody uses the feature.
- *Strong product management, weak project management:* the idea is great, but the release slips by three months and misses the festive season.

### Why this matters for a PM

- **Know which hat you are wearing.** In small teams you will switch between “what should we build?” and “will we finish on time?” several times a day.
- **Don’t confuse a plan with a strategy.** A perfectly scheduled project can still build the wrong product.
- **Expect overlap.** Especially in startups, PMs handle both disciplines. In large companies, they may be split into separate roles.
- **Interviews often ask this.** “What is the difference between a PM and a project manager?” is a common question.

### Key takeaways

1. Product management ensures **value**; project management ensures **feasibility**.
2. Product management faces **outward** (users, dependencies); project management faces **inward** (the team).
3. The PM translates needs into features; the project manager schedules, monitors, and reports.
4. One person can do both, and project management may sit with someone like an engineering manager.
5. Regardless of who does it, both disciplines must happen for a product to succeed.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

- ❌ “Product manager and project manager are the same role with different names.” They are different disciplines, even if one person covers both.
- ❌ “Every team must have both roles.” Many do not; the work is shared or combined.
- ❌ “If the project is on schedule, the product is succeeding.” On-time delivery says nothing about whether users want what was built.
- ❌ “Product management is only about ideas, project management is only about deadlines.” Product management also covers integration with other components, and project management also evaluates resources and budget.

### Self-test questions

1. In one sentence each, what question does product management answer, and what question does project management answer?
2. What does “outward” versus “inward” perspective mean in the circle model?
3. Why might an engineering manager end up doing project management?
4. Give an example of a product that was delivered on time and on budget but still failed. Which discipline was weak?
5. If you are a PM at a 10-person startup with no project manager, which responsibilities from both columns will you likely own?

## Lecture Notes: How the Industry Impacts Product Management

**Course:** Product Management: First Steps | **Topic:** Why the industry you work in shapes how you manage a product

### Summary

The PM’s core job (deciding what to build, then making sure it is designed, built, tested, and released) stays the same everywhere. But **how** that job is done changes a lot by industry. Three forces drive the difference: **regulation**, **how easily the product can be changed once building starts**, and **how costly it is to change direction (pivot)**.

### Core idea

> **Product management determines *what* is built. The industry determines *how* it can be built and *how easily* plans can change.**
> 

### The three factors that vary by industry

| Factor | What it means | High-constraint example | Low-constraint example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Regulation** | Legal and safety rules the product must follow | Automotive (airbags, seat belts, standard symbols) | Many consumer apps |
| **Cost of change during development** | How hard it is to alter the product once building has started | Cars (physical design locked early) | Software (changes made in code) |
| **Ease of pivoting** | How quickly the company can change direction | Fashion with seasonal cycles | Software startups |

### Industry deep dive 1: Automotive

**Characteristics**

- **Highly regulated.** Safety rules require features like airbags and seat belts, and some interface elements are standardized across all vehicles (e.g., the hazard button icon).
- **Design is locked early.** You must know from the start how every part fits and works together, and only then build the car.
- **Changes happen on a schedule.** Improvements arrive yearly through new model releases.

**Impact on the PM**

- Regulation changes force compliance work, which **takes time away from new features** customers want.
- Mid-build changes outside the model-year cycle **cause delays, create unforeseen problems, and put the product at risk.**
- Planning must be thorough upfront, because there is little room to adjust later.

### Industry deep dive 2: Software

**Characteristics**

- No riveting parts or wiring electronics. Everything is done through **code**.
- Features can be **changed readily during development**.

**Impact on the PM**

- Can test ideas quickly, release in small increments, and adjust based on feedback.
- Planning can be lighter because mistakes are cheaper to fix.

### Comparison: Car vs. Software

| Aspect | Automotive | Software |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Change during development | Very difficult | Relatively easy |
| Release rhythm | Yearly model updates | Can be continuous |
| Planning style | Heavy upfront design | Iterative |
| Regulatory load | High and constant | Varies (higher in finance, health) |
| Cost of a wrong decision | Very high | Lower |

### What is a pivot?

> **Pivot:** When something isn’t working, instead of cancelling and starting over, product management decides to **go in a different direction**.
> 
- **Easy in some industries.** Mobile, fast-moving tech companies can pivot quickly.
- **Painful in others.** Pivoting can be long, costly, and risky.

### Industry deep dive 3: Fashion (the pivot problem)

**The scenario from the lecture**

- An established company has made clothes for decades around **seasonal collections**: spring, summer, fall, holiday, winter.
- A competitor arrives and releases **new clothing every month or even every week**, giving customers a reason to keep returning.

**Why it’s a big problem for the incumbent**

- It is not just a product issue; it is an **operational process** issue (design cycles, manufacturing, supply chain, inventory).
- Adapting takes **time and money**, and puts the company **at risk** while it changes.

### Added examples *(not from the lecture)*

**Healthcare / medical devices**

A PM building a glucose monitor must pass regulatory approval and clinical testing before release. Launching a “quick beta” to see what happens is not an option. A software PM at a health-tech app faces fewer limits but must still follow data-privacy rules.

**Fintech (India context)**

A payments app PM must comply with RBI guidelines and NPCI rules for UPI. A new regulation can force an urgent roadmap change, similar to the car industry’s compliance burden, even though the product is software.

**Fast fashion in practice**

Brands built on rapid, frequent drops show what the lecture describes: their entire operation is designed for speed, so traditional seasonal brands struggle to match it without rebuilding their process.

**Pivot in software**

A startup builds a team-chat tool, finds users love one side feature (sharing files), and refocuses the whole product on that. This is feasible because the code and team can redirect quickly. A car maker cannot redirect a half-built vehicle line the same way.

### Why this matters for a PM

- **Adjust your approach to your industry.** Planning depth, release cadence, and risk tolerance should match the environment.
- **Budget for compliance.** In regulated industries, a share of every roadmap goes to meeting rules, not new features.
- **Know your pivot cost.** Before committing to a direction, understand how expensive it would be to reverse.
- **Watch competitors with different operating models.** A rival changing the rhythm of the game can expose your process, not just your product.
- **Skills transfer, context doesn’t.** Moving industries (say, software to automotive) means relearning constraints, even if the PM fundamentals remain.

### Key takeaways

1. The industry changes **how** a PM works, not **what** the PM is accountable for.
2. Regulation consumes time and shapes the roadmap, especially in industries like automotive.
3. Physical products usually need **heavy upfront planning**; software allows **faster change**.
4. A **pivot** is a change of direction; its cost varies enormously by industry.
5. Process changes (as in the fashion example) can be harder than product changes.
6. This course focuses mostly on technology products, but always consider your own industry’s quirks.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

- ❌ “Software is unregulated, so compliance never matters.” Finance, healthcare, and children’s products have heavy rules even in software.
- ❌ “Pivoting is always the right response when something fails.” It depends on the cost; in some industries it is extremely expensive.
- ❌ “Physical-product PMs cannot be agile or customer-focused.” They can, but changes must be planned around fixed cycles.
- ❌ “Competition is only about features.” As the fashion example shows, a competitor’s *operating process* can be the real threat.
