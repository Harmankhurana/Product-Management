## Lecture Notes: Developing a Strong Research Plan

**Course:** Product Management: First Steps | **Topic:** Organizing research so no corner is missed

### Summary

Research is easy to do badly: you finish, then discover you missed something important. The lecture’s fix is to treat research as an **organized process** shaped like a **square (a 2x2 grid)**. The **columns** are *how* you collect data (**quantitative** vs. **qualitative**). The **rows** are *where* the data comes from (**internal** vs. **external**). Covering all four quadrants gives you a well-balanced research plan.

### Core idea

> **Quantitative = data from many. Qualitative = depth from a few.**
> 
> 
> **Internal = your own data. External = data from outside your product or company.**
> 
> **A strong plan fills all four boxes.**
> 

### The research grid

|  | **Quantitative** (many people, standardized) | **Qualitative** (few people, in-depth) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Internal** (your own product or company) | Analytics on how people use your product | Interviews with your existing users |
| **External** (outside your product or company) | Large surveys of your target audience or market | Interviews with prospects who don’t use your product yet |

*The quadrant examples are mine, based on the lecture’s definitions. The lecture describes the axes but does not fill in every box.*

### The columns: how you collect data

#### Quantitative research

- Gets **a lot of data from a lot of people**.
- Uses **standardized questions and responses with no room for variation**.
- Produces data that **lines up to show trends** and gives feedback that can **direct your project**.
- Best for answering: *how many, how often, which option wins?*

**Three questions to ask when designing a survey**

| Question | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **How large is the population?** | The total group you want to learn about |
| **What margin of error is acceptable?** | How far the results can be from the true value (e.g., ±5%) |
| **What confidence level do you want?** | How sure you want to be the data is reliable (e.g., 95%) |

**The key trade-off:** The more accurate you want to be, the **more responses you need**. A calculator and formula exist for this. The lecture showed a table of starting numbers, which was not included in the transcript.

#### Qualitative research

- **In-depth information from a smaller group** who might use your product or have a problem it could solve.
- Adds **color**: emotional and personal preferences that **standardized surveys miss**.
- Uses **open-ended discussions**, so you can explore different areas and **aren’t limited to a fixed list of questions**.
- Best for answering: *why do people feel this way, and what do we not yet know to ask?*

### The rows: where data comes from

#### Internal data

- Data **you gathered yourself** during research for your product, plus analytics once the product is live.
- **Hard to get for a brand-new product**, since there are no users yet.
- Once in market, **analytics tools** show how people use it.
- Only **you** have access to it.

#### External data

- Information from **outside your product or company**.
- Used when you want to understand the needs of audience members **you have no internal data on**.

### Added reference: sample sizes for surveys *(not from the lecture)*

These use the standard formula at a **95% confidence level**. Use them as a starting point only.

| Population size | ±10% error | ±5% error | ±3% error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 50 | 80 | 92 |
| 1,000 | 88 | 278 | 517 |
| 10,000 | 96 | 370 | 965 |
| Very large (100,000+) | 97 | 385 | 1,068 |

**What to notice:**

- Tightening the margin of error from ±10% to ±3% roughly **multiplies the responses needed by 10 or more**.
- Beyond about 10,000 people, population size barely changes the sample you need.
- A small population needs a surprisingly **large share** of everyone (80 of 100 for ±5%).

### Example *(not from the lecture)*

**Researching a “scheduled orders” feature for a food delivery app**

|  | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Internal** | Analytics: what share of orders are placed between 12 and 2 pm, and how many users abandon carts at that time? | Call 8 existing customers who often order lunch late and ask them to walk you through a typical workday |
| **External** | Survey 400 office workers across the city about meal-planning habits (±5% margin, 95% confidence) | Interview 6 people who don’t use any delivery app, to learn why |

**What each quadrant catches**

- Analytics shows *what* users do. Interviews with existing users explain *why*.
- The external survey shows how big the opportunity is. The external interviews reveal barriers you would never see inside your own data.

**What goes wrong if a quadrant is missing**

- *Only internal data:* you only learn about people already using you and miss potential customers (this is survivorship bias).
- *Only quantitative:* you see the numbers but not the reasons, so you may fix the wrong problem.
- *Only qualitative:* a few vivid stories may not represent the market.

### Quantitative vs. qualitative at a glance

| Aspect | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sample size** | Large | Small |
| **Question style** | Standardized, closed | Open-ended, flexible |
| **Gives you** | Trends and patterns | Reasons, emotions, preferences |
| **Typical methods** | Surveys, analytics | Interviews, discussions |
| **Weakness** | Can miss the “why” | Hard to generalize |
| **Strength** | Reliable and measurable | Surfaces the unexpected |

### Why this matters for a PM

- **It prevents blind spots.** The grid is a checklist: if a box is empty, ask whether that gap matters.
- **Order often helps.** Qualitative research can reveal what to ask, and quantitative research can then measure how common it is.
- **New products lean external.** With no users yet, you rely on external data until you can collect internal data.
- **Decide survey size early.** Choose your margin of error and confidence level before launching a survey, not after.
- **Instrument your product.** Building analytics into your product creates future internal data (this links back to the Refine phase).
- **Interview relevance.** “How would you research whether to build feature X?” is a common question, and the 2x2 grid is a ready-made framework for the answer.

### Key takeaways

1. Research should be an **organized process**, so you avoid missing important areas.
2. **Quantitative** research collects standardized data from many people to show trends.
3. **Qualitative** research collects in-depth, open-ended input from a few people to capture emotion and nuance.
4. Surveys depend on **population size, margin of error, and confidence level**.
5. **Greater accuracy requires more responses.**
6. **Internal data** is your own (analytics, your own research); **external data** comes from outside your product or company.
7. Combining the two axes gives **four quadrants**, and a balanced plan covers all of them.

### Common mistakes and misconceptions

- ❌ “Quantitative research is better because it has bigger numbers.” Numbers show *what*, not *why*. You need both.
- ❌ “Qualitative research is unscientific.” It surfaces insights that surveys structurally cannot capture.
- ❌ “More survey responses are always needed.” The right number depends on your margin of error and confidence level.
- ❌ “Internal data is enough.” It only describes people already using your product.
- ❌ “New products have no data.” They have no internal usage data yet, so rely on external research.
- ❌ “Margin of error and confidence level mean the same thing.” Margin of error is how close the result is to the truth; confidence level is how sure you are of that range.

## Lecture Note: Research Plan Examples

### Core Idea

A well-balanced research plan is built on two axes:

1. **Type of data:** quantitative (numbers, measurable) versus qualitative (descriptive, opinion- and context-based)
2. **Source of data:** internal (from inside your app or company) versus external (from outside it)

Crossing these two axes gives four quadrants, and a good plan draws from all four.

|  | Internal | External |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quantitative** | App analytics | Surveys |
| **Qualitative** | Customer interviews or meetings | Industry or analyst reports |

### The Scenario Used in the Transcript

A private family group chat app. The team has been making small improvements, but the app is performing poorly in the app store, and they need to decide what to build in the next major version.

### The Four Quadrants

#### 1. Quantitative Internal Data

- **Source:** Anonymous analytics tools inside the app.
- **What it shows:** Which sections are used, which features are used, and how long people use the app.
- **Example finding:** Some sections are barely used, or users try a certain feature and then immediately open the Help link.
- **Key point:** This data does not tell you definitively why people behave this way. It gives you questions to investigate with more data.

#### 2. Quantitative External Data

- **Source:** A survey sent to people outside your current user base.
- **Why it fits here:** The app has few users, so there is a large pool of potential customers to learn from.
- **Example finding:** Parents and children often use different platforms, or children want emoji support.
- **Key point:** Going outside your app or company to collect measurable data makes it external.

#### 3. Qualitative Internal Data

- **Source:** Customer interviews or meetings.
- **Why it fits here:** Some questions need a real conversation to uncover specifics that numbers cannot capture.

#### 4. Qualitative External Data

- **Source:** Industry or analyst reports.
- **Why it is qualitative:** These reports share trends and indicators rather than raw survey-style numbers.
- **Example finding:** The age at which children get their first phone has dropped sharply in recent years, which might inspire a new feature.

### How the Quadrants Work Together

Each quadrant raises new questions that can be checked in another quadrant. For example, a survey might reveal a trend, an interview can confirm it, and an industry report or internal analytics can verify it further. This cross-checking is what makes the plan reliable.

### Additional Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: A food delivery app that is losing repeat customers

- **Quantitative internal:** Order data shows that 60 percent of new users never place a second order.
- **Quantitative external:** A survey of people who deleted the app finds that many cite high delivery fees.
- **Qualitative internal:** Interviews with current customers reveal they feel confused by how the fees are calculated.
- **Qualitative external:** An industry report shows that competitors are moving toward subscription plans with free delivery.

Together, these suggest the next version should address fee transparency and possibly introduce a subscription option.

#### Example 2: A language learning app with low engagement

- **Quantitative internal:** Analytics show most users quit during the third lesson.
- **Quantitative external:** A survey of language learners shows most prefer short 5-minute sessions.
- **Qualitative internal:** Support conversations reveal that lesson three introduces grammar too suddenly.
- **Qualitative external:** An analyst report notes a rise in learners choosing speaking practice over grammar drills.

This points toward shorter lessons, a gentler grammar introduction, and more conversation features.

#### Example 3: What goes wrong with an unbalanced plan

If the family chat team only looked at internal analytics, they would see what users do but never learn why, and they would miss the needs of people who have not downloaded the app yet. Relying on only one quadrant leaves blind spots, which is why the balanced grid is a good starting point.

## Plan a Product Research Customer Meeting

### Core Idea

A customer meeting is one part of a larger research plan. It lets you go deeper than a survey and lets the conversation wander a little, but you still need a plan so that you leave with actionable feedback: what the customer wants and when they want it. The meetings are the qualitative internal quadrant from the previous notes.

The participant can be:

- An end user, or
- A decision-maker who handles purchasing for a larger group

### Why Meet Customers Face to Face

- You can dig deeper than a survey allows.
- The conversation can meander, but only to a point.
- The output, combined with your other research, gives you a strong start for planning the product.

### The Four Prep Steps

#### 1. Define who is on the core team

- Choose a few people who take part in every meeting, discussion, and step of the process.
- This group is often called the **Discovery Team** or **Tiger Team**. The name does not matter.
- Recommended members: the product manager plus a representative each from user experience, engineering, and marketing.

#### 2. Define who you want to meet

- Think in categories: specific industries, demographics, or potential customers.
- Aim for three to five categories.
- Use this list as a guide so meetings are spread equally across all groups.

#### 3. Agree on the critical questions

- Decide which questions must be answered by the end of the interview process.
- Keep the number small at first, about five to seven.
- Make them specific so you can design the discussion to produce a clear answer.

#### 4. Schedule the meetings

- How you schedule depends on the customer type and product.
- Use your categories to keep the mix of people balanced.

### Running the Meeting

- Set an agenda before you hold the meeting.
- You should not be doing most of the talking. Your job is to listen, so structure the discussion to get the customer speaking.
- Invite all members of the core team.
- Assign one person as the **scribe**, who writes down almost everything the customer says for later reference.
- The scribe usually does not take part in the conversation.
- Rotate the scribe role across meetings so everyone gets a turn.

### Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: A project management tool for small businesses

**Core team:** product manager, a UX designer, a backend engineer, and a marketing specialist.

**Customer categories (four groups):**

- Freelancers
- Small agencies with fewer than 20 people
- Construction companies
- Operations managers at larger firms who decide on software purchases

**Critical questions (five):**

1. How do they currently track deadlines?
2. What is the most frustrating part of their current tool?
3. Who in their organization approves a new software purchase?
4. Which feature would make them switch tools?
5. How soon would they need such a feature?

**Scheduling:** Two meetings per category, so the final eight conversations are balanced rather than dominated by one group.

#### Example 2: An agenda that keeps the customer talking

Instead of opening with a product demo, a 45-minute meeting could be structured as:

- 5 minutes: introductions and explaining the purpose
- 25 minutes: open questions such as “Walk me through the last time you tried to coordinate a project”
- 10 minutes: follow-up on the critical questions not yet covered
- 5 minutes: thank them and ask if they would join a follow-up

Here the customer speaks for most of the time, which is the goal.

#### Example 3: The scribe in action

In the first meeting, the UX representative is scribe and records quotes such as “I spend every Monday rebuilding my task list by hand.” The engineer takes the role in the second meeting, and so on. When the team reviews later, the notes show a repeated pain point across several customers rather than one person’s memory of the conversation.

#### Example 4: What goes wrong without planning

Suppose a team only meets with friendly existing customers, has no fixed questions, and the product manager talks for most of the hour. They end up with pleasant chat, no clear answers on what customers want or when, and a one-sided view of the market. Defining categories, critical questions, and the listening structure prevents this.

## Open a Product Research Customer Meeting

### Core Idea

The opening of a customer meeting sets the tone for everything that follows. The goal is to keep the focus on the customer, give them room to share, and still guide the conversation toward information your team can act on. A good opening also builds a strong relationship, so the customer feels their feedback is valued.

### The Four Steps of a Strong Opening

#### 1. Introduce all participants

- Introduce everyone on your team and explain how each person relates to the others and to the project.
- This helps the customer know who they are talking to.
- It also lets them anticipate what kinds of questions each person might ask and how to answer.

#### 2. Let the customer introduce themselves

- Invite them to talk about who they are and what they do.
- Do not let them jump into likes and dislikes about the product yet. That comes later.

#### 3. Let the customer vent

- Give them time to say everything that is on their mind.
- It is cathartic, and customers are usually calmer and more constructive afterward.
- It also gives your team a chance to establish an active listening habit right at the start.

#### 4. Guide from venting to focused reaction

- Move the customer from freeform comments toward the topics you want to discuss.
- Put something in front of them to react to, usually a set of statements.
- Use a mix of positive and negative statements related to what you want to pitch.
- Present them as quotes and ask whether the customer agrees or disagrees.
- Let this flow naturally into the next phase of the meeting.

### Why This Order Works

Customers usually arrive with feedback already formed in their minds, and it may not match the conversation you want to have. Letting them release it first clears the way. The reaction statements then channel their attention to the areas that matter for your research.

### Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: Introductions

A team meeting a restaurant owner about a new reservation system might say:

“I’m Priya, the product manager. Arjun is our designer and works on how the screens look and feel. Meera is our engineer and can answer how things work behind the scenes. Sam is taking notes and will mostly be listening.”

The owner now knows that questions about layout, technical limits, and business needs may come from different people.

#### Example 2: Letting the customer introduce themselves

Prompt: “Could you tell us about your restaurant and what a typical day looks like for you?”

If the owner starts with “Honestly, the booking screen is terrible,” the facilitator can say, “We definitely want to hear that, and we will get there shortly. First, tell us more about your day.” This keeps the order intact without dismissing the customer.

#### Example 3: Letting them vent

After the introductions, the facilitator says, “What has been on your mind about the current system?” The owner talks for ten minutes about double bookings and customers calling to cancel. The team does not interrupt or defend the product. By the end, the owner sounds calmer and begins offering specific suggestions.

#### Example 4: Reaction statements

For the same reservation system, the team could show these cards:

- “I get frustrated when two parties are booked for the same table.” (negative)
- “I find it easy to see which tables are free during a busy evening.” (positive)
- “I would rather have guests manage their own cancellations.” (idea to test)
- “Setting up a new table layout takes too long.” (negative)

The customer says whether they agree or disagree and explains why. A response like “I agree with the first and the last, but I never use the table view” gives focused, usable feedback that flows into the next part of the discussion.

#### Example 5: What goes wrong with a poor opening

Suppose the team skips introductions, immediately starts a product demo, and cuts the customer off when they complain. The customer feels unheard, holds back honest opinions, and the team leaves with surface-level feedback and a weaker relationship.

## Pitch Your Product Idea to the Customer

### Core Idea

The pitch is the heart of the customer meeting. You are in the room to find out whether you are on the right track or need to change direction. How you pitch depends on the product type, and the pitch must be followed by a structured evaluation so the customer can tell you what they want and what they do not.

The pitch can take the form of:

- A mockup of an app
- A prototype of a device
- A description of a process or idea

It can be an entirely new product or an improvement to an existing one.

### The Golden Rule: Do Not Answer Their Questions

When a customer asks a question, redirect it back to them instead of answering.

- If you answer, the customer may not react at all.
- If they do react, you have led them with your answer, so their feedback may differ from what they would have said on their own.
- If you turn the question around, you get an honest, unsolicited response.

**Transcript example:** A customer asks, “How much storage will be on the phone?” A better reply is, “How much storage would you expect to get on the phone?”

### Structuring the Pitch

- Organize the features into individual sections, slides, or parts.
- Give each one a title or name you can refer to later.
- These titles tie the pitch to the evaluation, since the customer will use them to say what they want and what they do not.

### The $100 Test (Evaluation)

#### The setup

1. List all features or capabilities of your product.
2. Add a couple of blank lines for features the customer may have requested earlier in the conversation.
3. Tell the customer they have $100 to spend across the features.

#### Giving context for the spending levels

- **$5:** The feature exists but is very simple and elementary.
- **$20:** The feature is well thought out and well developed.
- **$50:** The feature is best in class and better than the competition, one the customer will love.

#### Optional conditions

Adding rules forces the customer to think critically. For example, with five features and $100:

- The minimum investment per feature is $5.
- The customer must eliminate one feature entirely.

This makes them decide what they are willing to live without, which helps you focus on what to build.

#### The result

- A clear ranked list of which features customers value over others.
- A model you can compare across multiple customer meetings to spot trends and similarities.

### Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: Redirecting questions in a meal-planning app pitch

- Customer: “Will this app work offline?”
- Weak reply: “Yes, it will.”
- Better reply: “How important would it be for you to use it offline, and in what situations?”

The customer might then say, “I shop at a market with no signal, so I would need my list there.” That is far more useful than a simple yes or no reaction.

#### Example 2: Titled pitch sections for a meal-planning app

1. Weekly Planner
2. Smart Shopping List
3. Recipe Suggestions
4. Pantry Tracker
5. Nutrition Summary

Later, the facilitator can say, “Let’s go back to Pantry Tracker,” and the customer knows exactly what is meant.

#### Example 3: A $100 test result

One customer in the meal-planning meeting allocates:

| Feature | Investment |
| --- | --- |
| Weekly Planner | $20 |
| Smart Shopping List | $50 |
| Recipe Suggestions | $15 |
| Pantry Tracker | $0 (eliminated) |
| Nutrition Summary | $5 |
| Blank line: Share list with family | $10 |

Total: $100. The customer clearly values the shopping list, wants a simple nutrition summary, does not care about the pantry tracker, and added a feature the team had not planned.

#### Example 4: Comparing across meetings

After six meetings, the team averages the results:

- Smart Shopping List: average $42
- Weekly Planner: average $22
- Pantry Tracker: average $4, and eliminated by four of six customers

The trend shows where to focus development and which feature may be cut or postponed.

#### Example 5: What goes wrong without these techniques

If the team answers every question, hands over a feature list, and simply asks, “Do you like these?”, the customer will probably say yes to everything. The team learns nothing about priorities. The $100 test forces trade-offs and exposes what really matters.

## Summarize Customer Meeting Findings

### Core Idea

A customer meeting is not finished when the customer leaves. Much of what you learned will fade or get distorted if you do not capture it right away. The best practice is to sit down as a team immediately afterward, summarize what was heard, and use those findings to improve the next round of meetings.

### Step 1: Debrief Immediately as a Group

- Do not discuss the meeting individually or informally before the group sits down together. Early side conversations can shape people’s memories and bias the summary.
- Ask each team member the same basic questions:
    - What was the worst thing you heard?
    - What was the best thing you heard?
    - What went well in the meeting?
    - What did not go well?
- These questions give structure to everyone’s responses and help guide your approach to the next customer.

### Step 2: Build the Meeting Record

At the end of each debrief, you should have a complete list of:

1. The customer’s vents about the product
2. Their reactions, statements, and opinions about the product
3. Unsolicited responses to your product features
4. A prioritization of what matters most to them (for example, from the $100 test)
5. Your team’s summary of what worked and what did not

### Step 3: Look Across Multiple Meetings

After a few meetings with different customers:

- Summarize the combined findings.
- Use them to adjust how you run the next group of meetings.
- Compare $100 test results to see whether trends emerge.
- Look for explanations when a feature gets little investment. The cause may be the feature itself, or it may be how you described it.
- Decide whether to change your pitch or drop features that people do not want.

### Step 4: Return to Your Critical Questions

- Check how well each critical question was answered.
- It is fine if some are not fully answered.
- Plan the next round of meetings so the unanswered questions get covered.

### What You Gain After Several Rounds

- Features you believed were must-haves may turn out to be duds.
- Features suggested by customers may become the highlight.
- You become confident in your findings and add valuable information to your overall product research.

### Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: A debrief for a fitness tracking app

Right after the meeting, the team gathers and answers the four questions:

- **Worst thing heard:** “I stopped using the app because it made me feel guilty about missed workouts.”
- **Best thing heard:** “The weekly summary is the only reason I still open it.”
- **What went well:** The customer spoke most of the time and gave detailed stories.
- **What did not go well:** The pitch of the “Challenges” feature was unclear, and the customer asked three questions about it.

Notice how the last point is about the team’s performance, not only the customer’s opinions.

#### Example 2: Spotting a trend across meetings

After four meetings, the team compares $100 test results:

| Feature | Customer 1 | Customer 2 | Customer 3 | Customer 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly Summary | $40 | $35 | $45 | $30 |
| Challenges | $5 | $0 | $10 | $5 |
| Social Sharing | $25 | $30 | $20 | $35 |

Challenges received very little money. Before dropping it, the team reviews its debrief notes and sees that the feature was confusing in the pitch. They rewrite the description and test it again with the next group. If it still gets little investment, they will drop it with confidence.

#### Example 3: Revisiting critical questions

The team’s critical question was, “Would users pay for a premium plan?” After four meetings, nobody was asked about pricing because time ran short. For the next round, the team adds a dedicated pricing discussion near the end of each meeting so the question gets answered.

#### Example 4: A surprise from a customer suggestion

During the first meeting, a customer suggests a feature to sync workouts with a smartwatch. The team had not planned it, so they add it as a blank line in the next $100 test. Three out of the next five customers invest heavily in it, and it becomes one of the top priorities.

#### Example 5: What goes wrong without a prompt debrief

Suppose the team waits a week before talking. One person remembers the customer being happy, another remembers frustration, and the specific quotes are gone. The team ends up arguing from vague impressions rather than evidence, and the next meeting repeats the same mistakes.

## Present Your Customer Meeting Findings

### Core Idea

At the end of each phase of the product lifecycle, there is a deliverable that summarizes everything learned so it can be carried into the next phase. It documents that point in time so you can refer back to it later. Because research produces a lot of data, you need a clear strategy for organizing it before you present it.

### Two Strategies for Organizing Findings

#### Strategy 1: Organize by the four quadrants of feedback

- Open with the theme of this round of research.
- Show how each quadrant (quantitative internal, quantitative external, qualitative internal, qualitative external) broadened your understanding of that theme.
- **Advantage:** It shows a balanced view and proves you looked at the theme from multiple angles before deciding what to build.

#### Strategy 2: Organize by the critical questions

- Use the critical questions defined before the customer meetings as your structure.
- Answer each one using evidence from all your sources.
- **Advantage:** If you can answer the questions from multiple sources, you can present solid findings with confidence.

### Choosing a Format

#### Internal white paper

- Gives a full narrative and documentation of the audience, their reactions to the product, and their responses to feature pitches.
- Can be delivered as a document or through a collaborative wiki or document service.
- **Drawback:** Can be too long for people with limited time.

#### Slide presentation

- Works well for live delivery, where you provide the narrative that connects the pieces.
- To extend its reach, record yourself giving the presentation with screen recording software.
- The recording becomes a self-contained presentation with the context needed to share the complete vision for the product.

### Whichever Format You Choose

1. Provide a lead into the plan phase by explaining how your research will help you plan the product.
2. Review which features resonated most with customers for future development.

The deliverable gives stakeholders connection points, so they can see where each phase is going and what is carried over from earlier ones.

### Examples for Better Understanding

#### Example 1: Presenting by quadrant

Theme: “Why do new users of our budgeting app stop after the first week?”

- **Quantitative internal:** Analytics show 55 percent of new users never return after day seven.
- **Quantitative external:** A survey of budgeting app users finds that most abandon apps that require manual entry of every expense.
- **Qualitative internal:** Customer interviews reveal people feel overwhelmed by the setup process.
- **Qualitative external:** An analyst report shows competitors are adding automatic bank syncing.

Conclusion: The next version should simplify setup and reduce manual entry.

#### Example 2: Presenting by critical questions

For the same budgeting app, the team’s critical questions were:

1. What makes users quit in the first week?
    - Answer: Heavy setup and manual entry, supported by analytics, survey, and interviews.
2. Which features matter most?
    - Answer: Automatic expense categorization averaged $38 in the $100 test, the highest of any feature.
3. Would users pay for a premium plan?
    - Answer: Four of six customers said yes, but only for bank syncing and reports.

Each answer cites more than one source, which makes the findings credible.

#### Example 3: Choosing the right format

- A product director who wants the full detail on customer reactions would receive the **white paper** on the company wiki.
- A cross-team meeting with engineering, marketing, and sales would be a **live slide presentation**.
- Executives who cannot attend the meeting would receive the **recorded version** of that presentation, so they get the full story at their own pace.

#### Example 4: The lead into the plan phase

The final slide of the budgeting app presentation might read:

“Based on this research, the plan phase will focus on three things: a simplified setup flow, automatic expense categorization, and a roadmap for bank syncing. Features such as custom report themes, which received almost no investment, will be postponed.”

This shows stakeholders exactly how the research connects to the next phase.

#### Example 5: What goes wrong with a poor presentation

Suppose the team delivers a 40-page document with raw interview notes and every survey chart, with no theme or conclusions. Stakeholders skim the first pages, miss the key findings, and cannot see how the research leads to a product plan. Organizing around a theme or critical questions, and ending with a clear lead into the plan phase, avoids this.
